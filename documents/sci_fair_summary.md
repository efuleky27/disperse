Title (400): 
Disentangling the Cosmic Web: The Relationship between Walls and Other Cosmic Structures

Short Title (150):


Gravity and expansion shapes the universe into a vast cosmic web of empty regions (voids), flat sheets (walls), thin threads (filaments), and dense clusters. While filaments and clusters are well studied, walls are harder to detect and therefore less understood. Because walls and filaments form at different stages of gravitational collapse, comparing their density and spatial relationship can improve our understanding of how dark matter organizes large-scale cosmic structures. My project automates the full workflow of extracting, analyzing, and visualizing the cosmic web (clusters, filaments, walls) from cosmological snapshots using the Discrete Persistent Structures Extractor (DisPerSE). The pipeline splits the data into tiled volumes of manageable size, executes the components of DisPerSE (Delaunay Tessellation Field Estimator and Morse–Smale Topology Extractor), produces per-crop statistics, 2D/3D visualizations, and aggregates results across the tiles. Results show that gravitational collapse has made these structures more dense over time and they have become more pronounced in visualizations. The share of particles associated with each of these structures has also increased between redshifts z=3 and z=0, while the number of particles not assigned to any of these categories has declined. While clusters have become more dense overall, the density distribution in the other categories has widened, mostly by extending towards higher-density values. These findings are the first step in allowing us to understand the material skeleton of the universe and the gravitational backbone shaped by dark matter. Future research should aim to look at the cosmic web using different cosmological models.




INTRODUCTION:

Look up at night, and you’ll find stars and galaxies scattered across the sky. Interestingly, they have been found to follow a pattern: they are part of a large-scale cosmic web. The cosmic web is shaped by gravity, pulling and pushing material into strands showing that our universe isn’t as homogenous as previously believed. Walls are fundamental structures in this web, along with clusters, filaments, and voids. Looking at this web as a sponge or a collection of bubbles: clusters are collections of galaxies; filaments connect clusters together; voids are the empty region where matter is sparse; walls are sheets of matter surrounding voids (Large Scale Structures - NASA Science). In our observable universe, we have found many large scale structures using galaxy redshift surveys with the largest being Quipu (a filament) and Hercules-Corona Borealis Great Wall (Bassi, 2025). 
This project recreates the cosmic web using simulations that replicate the physics that govern our universe. The project automates the full workflow of extracting, analyzing, and visualizing the cosmic web (walls, filaments, clusters) from cosmological snapshots using the Discrete Persistent Structures Extractor (DisPerSE). The pipeline splits the data into tiled volumes of manageable size, executes the components of DisPerSE (Delaunay Tessellation Field Estimator and Morse–Smale Topology Extractor), produces per-crop statistics, 2D/3D visualizations, and aggregates results across the tiles. This project also addresses an issue that arises during the visualization of these structures (Sousbie, 2013). In 3D, walls are sheets while filaments are thin threads. When such structures are projected onto a 2D plane, it is difficult to distinguish between them.
Being able to identify filaments and walls in the universe allows researchers to better understand the distribution of not only baryonic material but also dark matter. After the big bang, dark matter collapsed first, allowing gas to cool and form stars. Dark matter continued collapsing creating the structure that we now see and baryonic material followed. Being able to distinguish between the two structures noted above, filaments and walls, allows us to better understand the fluctuations created at the beginning of the universe, the distribution of dark matter and potentially characterize it (Large Scale Structure | Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian). 

RESEARCH QUESTIONS:
What types of visualizations can be used to differentiate between filaments and walls? 
How do filaments and walls differ in density and spatial distribution?
Can we observe in the data the gravitational collapse that gives rise to these structures?

METHODS:
I carried out the analysis on a cosmological simulation, which I obtained from the Quijote simulations. In order to transfer data onto the laptop, Quijote required Globus as the bridge. The specific data I used in the analysis are two fiducial N-body simulations that contain the redshift 3 and redshift 0 snapshots, the universe 11.5 billion years ago and at the present moment, respectively. The simulation size is 1 Gpc/h. The parameters for this fiducial model are consistent with Planck constraints. Next, with the help of ChatGPT, I wrote a preliminary python script in VS Code to execute a DisPerSE (Discrete Persistent Structures Extractor) workflow. DisPerSE is a set of command line tools for identifying various topologies in 3D simulations. 
Breaking down the workflow:
Step 1: Data Acquisition and Volume Tiling. The first step is converting the data from the hdf5 files obtained from Quijote into ASCII format which DisPerSE can ingest. However, the memory on the laptop (16GB RAM) was insufficient to handle all 134 million particles during the analysis. To resolve this issue, I used python to decrease the sample size (down-sample). I tried 2 methods for this: thinning the data by giving a target count and subsampling (take every nth particle) or maintaining the resolution but cropping the dimensions of the data by providing a range (a minimum and maximum) for each axis. I ended up using the second of these and cycling through the whole original 1 Gpc/h simulation in increments. This method resulted in 40 cropped boxes with a particle count of around 3.5 million per crop (crop sizes: 500 Mpc x 500 Mpc x100 Mpc).
Step 2: Delaunay Tessellation and Density Estimation. Next, the python script calls the Delaunay_3D module of DisPerSE, which generates a tessellation for discrete particle distributions. The output is a NDnet file which contains density computed for each particle using Delaunay Tessellation Field Estimator (the density field is found by linearly interpolating along the surrounding tessellation) using periodic boundary conditions. The resulting density field serves as the input for subsequent topological analysis with the Morse–Smale complex extractor (MSE). No smoothing is applied at this stage, so that the tessellation represents a lossless geometric reconstruction of the density field.
Step 3: Morse–Smale Topology Extraction. Next, the python script calls MSE to perform topological feature extraction on the Delaunay network. The analysis is done with a persistence ratio (significance threshold) set to 3.5 sigmas for redshift z=3 and 6.0 sigmas for redshift z=0. These thresholds differ because at z=0, gravitational collapse has had more time to act, amplifying density contrasts throughout the cosmic web; as a result, more topological features form in the density field, and a higher sigma threshold is needed to retain only the most significant structures while discarding noise. I tested sigma values in 0.5 sigma increments, and anything higher than those chosen sigma values crashed MSE because too many features got suppressed. The manifolds (walls and filaments) were exported into an NDnet file for further analysis, with the dumpManifolds option specifying which information was included. Option J(oin) combines all data into a single output file. Option E(xtended) includes boundaries in the export. Option 1 captures walls, which originate from critical points with index 1, and option 2 captures filaments, which originate from critical points with index 2. Option a(scending) captures walls and filaments, which are represented by ascending manifolds. The arcs (a second approach to obtain filaments) were exported into an NDskl file with the dumpArcs set to U(p) to include all arcs leading to maxima (filaments).
Step 4: Format Conversion and Coordinate Restoration. Next, the python script calls the DisPerSE converter modules that take the output files from MSE (NDnet and NDskl format) and convert them into vtk files that can be visualized using Paraview. All outputs were shifted back to their original coordinates using the stored crop origin in order to have spatial consistency (the crop in step 1 shifted the coordinates of the data). Paraview is an open source software with a python interface used for visualizing and analyzing datasets. It allows manifolds (walls), arcs (filaments), and the density fields to be visualized in 3D or flattened versions in 2D, and saved in PNG format.
Step 5: 2D Slab Visualization. Next, the python script slices the crops to thinner layers along a chosen axis (e.g., the z-axis), in order to visualize the wall, filament, and density at a more granular level. The main arguments for this operation are the slice origin coordinates and its thickness (e.g. 10 Mpc/h). The wall and filament geometries are then flattened onto the xy-axis plane by collapsing the thickness to 0. I also saved density fields by averaging across the block’s thickness and resampling onto a regular grid with specified dimensions. The scalar field used for coloring the visualizations is the logarithm of the density. I save the PNG images of the 3D blocks and flattened 2D projections of walls, filaments, and density fields. Some of the images include layers of filament and wall structures on top of the density field. Because walls are 2D manifolds, they appear as broad, diffuse sheets in the projection, while filaments appear as narrow, elongated lines — making the two structure types visually distinguishable in the same image even when spatially co-located.
Step 6: Per-Particle Classification and Statistics. Structures are matched back to the original particles. Once each particle is properly defined, Delaunay scalar quantities are aggregated across four topology categories: clusters (local maxima of the density field), filaments (1D saddle paths), wall-only regions (2D manifolds), and particles not assigned to either of the previously noted structures. The resulting output files show summary statistics for each category (mean, minimum, quarter 25, median, quarter 75, maximum). The statistics are collected for each slab and for the entire cube, which is then converted into histograms, barcharts (for total count), violin plots, and box plots.

DATA ANALYSIS:
Being able to overlay the filaments on top of walls allows identification of what characteristics (such as density) is more prominent in a certain structure over the other. Additionally, the layering of all 3 (filaments, walls, and density) shows the background of where the filaments run and walls lie. 
The quantitative analysis reveals how density is distributed across the distinct structural components. The analysis shows that particles representing clusters are relatively rare, but they are very dense compared to other structures. Comparing density distributions across redshifts indicates that gravitational collapse is pulling material to denser regions. The density of filaments and walls has taken on a wider distribution over time, which implies that material has “clumped” together within these structures. This “clumping” led to material being distributed more unevenly, resulting in more heterogeneous composition of the structures.

The slab visualization approach—slicing each crop to a thickness of 10 Mpc and projecting the retained structures onto a 2D plane—directly addresses research question 1 by making it possible to visually distinguish walls from filaments within the same image. Because walls are 2D manifolds, they appear as broad, diffuse sheets when flattened onto the projection plane. Filaments, being 1D structures, appear as narrow, elongated lines threading through or across those sheets. Overlaying both structure types on top of the log-density field further reinforces the contrast: walls tend to coincide with extended regions of moderate density elevation, while filaments trace the highest-density ridges within those regions. This confirms that 2D projected slabs are an effective visualization strategy for separating the two structure types.

The redshift comparison plots—violin plots, box plots, and scatter plots of log-density against particle proximity and Voronoi cell volume—address research questions 2 and 3. Research question 2 asks how filaments and walls differ in density and spatial distribution: the violin and box plots show that filaments occupy a higher and narrower density range than walls at both redshifts, and the scatter of log-density against particle proximity reveals that filament particles tend to be closer together, consistent with their more compact, thread-like geometry. Research question 3 asks whether gravitational collapse is observable in the data: comparing the same plots between z=3 and z=0 shows that all structure categories shift toward higher densities over time, the share of unassigned particles decreases, and the distributions of filaments and walls widen toward higher-density values—all signatures of ongoing gravitational collapse concentrating matter into the cosmic web.

Specifically for filaments, it was found that more than 99.9% of identified filaments are embedded within walls, demonstrating that filaments rarely exist as independent features and instead function primarily as substructures within larger sheet-like formations. Those filaments, found inside of walls, also tended to be denser, suggesting filaments form inside of a larger structure, in this case walls. This can also be applied to clusters. Nearly all clusters are found inside of filaments with very few exceptions. Those located inside have a significantly higher density than those clusters located outside of filaments.

CONCLUSIONS:
Research question 1 asked what types of visualizations can be used to differentiate between filaments and walls. The slab visualization approach, which slices the simulation volume to 10 Mpc thickness and projects structures onto a 2D plane, proved effective: walls appear as broad sheets while filaments appear as narrow lines, making the two structure types visually distinguishable in the same image even when they are spatially co-located.

Research question 2 asked how filaments and walls differ in density and spatial distribution. The analysis shows that filaments occupy a higher and narrower density range than walls, and that more than 99.9% of identified filaments are embedded within walls, demonstrating that filaments function primarily as substructures within larger sheet-like formations. Filaments found inside walls are also denser than those found outside, and nearly all clusters are found inside filaments, indicating a clear hierarchy of density from walls to filaments to clusters.

Research question 3 asked whether gravitational collapse can be observed in the data. Comparing snapshots at z=3 and z=0 reveals that all structure categories have shifted toward higher densities over time, the share of particles assigned to structures has increased while unassigned particles have declined, and the density distributions of filaments and walls have widened toward higher values. These trends are consistent with ongoing gravitational collapse progressively concentrating matter into the cosmic web over the 11.5 billion years separating the two snapshots.

Together, these findings confirm that the automated DisPerSE pipeline successfully reconstructs and quantifies the large-scale cosmic web, that walls and filaments form a nested hierarchy ordered by density, and that the data contain a clear observational signature of gravitational collapse driving the evolution of cosmic structure from z=3 to z=0.
